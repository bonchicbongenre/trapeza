What itsthefloor.fyi does. A golf pass must keep every line true.

 1. A new continuous floor on every visit, and on "another floor": bumps, waves,
    a hill, or a saddle.
 2. The plan shows the floor in three celadon washes by height (low qingbai, middle
    Ru, high Longquan) with faint ink contours every 0.012 of a foot's reach.
 3. The table is a regular top with 3, 4, 5 or 6 feet, chosen by the leg buttons
    (the chosen one is pressed). Feet sit at the corners' directions, at radius 1;
    the top reaches 1.12.
 4. Dragging turns it, as do the arrow keys (half a degree a press). Turning eases.
 5. The table rests on a triangle of feet that leaves every foot on or above the
    floor and holds the centre. With two such triangles it rocks between them as a
    leaned-on table does: smoothly, lingering a little at each rest. The page's
    clock runs at five sixths of real time, so everything happens as it would, a
    little slower: about 2.3 s there and back, a fall from the table about 0.28 s. With reduced motion it rests on the first. No foot ever goes
    below the floor.
 6. From above, the top is shaded by its tilt: a light wash on the high side,
    darker and more opaque toward the low side, deeper with steeper tilt, never
    opaque. A foot is filled when it touches, hollow when it is more than 0.004
    above the floor in either resting state.
 7. Two cameras, a and b, each a short line and a letter on the plan, on the side
    it looks from; they turn with the table. Their elevations show the floor's
    section at every foot's depth, back to front, each foot on its own ground; the
    legs as square posts perpendicular to the top, leaning with its tilt, the foot's
    bottom showing when the table tips away; the top as an opaque slab showing its
    top or its underside, whichever faces the camera. Grounds, legs and marbles are
    drawn back to front, and each is hidden by exactly the part of the slab nearer
    the camera than it, so nothing appears or vanishes except by moving. Heights are
    drawn at twice their size, and the table is one rigid piece in that drawing. The ground slices belong to the feet, so the floor
    holds still; a marble on the floor carries a short line of its own ground.
 8. A screen reader hears "It is still.", "It rocks.", "One foot is in the air."
    or "N feet are in the air."
 9. The label gives the page's own size, read live, and one line: "Turn it." The
    title (the tab, and any preview of a link) is "It's not your table that's
    wobbly." The console gives the Greek
    word for table, and what it means.
10. Colours come from the house tokens; a missing token falls back to ink or
    paper. Dark mode follows the system. Resizing redraws.
11. Marbles, at true size (16 mm, a reach taken as 40 cm). Clicking the plan drops
    one just there, onto the top or the floor; "drop a marble" drops one somewhere
    on the top. Each drop adds one. A marble rolls downhill at five-sevenths of
    gravity times the slope, against rolling resistance (half a percent of
    gravity) that holds it still on a slope gentler than about 0.4 degrees; it
    rides with the table when the table turns, falls off the edge onto the floor,
    rolls on until it rests in a hollow or leaves the map. Marbles knock into one
    another. A marble is Jun blue on the table and Jun crimson once it has left
    it, in every view. A screen reader hears how many are on the table, rolling,
    resting, and gone.
12. Each elevation is a camera with a frame: nothing in it is drawn outside its
    panel, and a marble out of shot is simply not in that view.
13. The favicon is the table from above, in amber, lighter on dark tab bars.
