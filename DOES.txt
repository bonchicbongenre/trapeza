What itsthefloor.fyi does. A golf pass must keep every line true.

 1. A new continuous floor on every visit, and on "another floor": bumps, waves,
    a hill, or a saddle.
 2. The plan shows the floor in three washes by height, all of one family: celadon
    at first (low qingbai, middle Ru, high Longquan), then Jun (crimson, purple
    splash, Jun blue), then tenmoku (gold, amber, rust); with faint ink contours
    every 0.012 of a foot's reach.
 3. The table is a regular top with 3, 4, 5 or 6 feet, chosen by the leg buttons
    (the chosen one is pressed). Feet sit at the corners' directions, at radius 1;
    the top reaches 1.12.
 4. Dragging turns it, as do the arrow keys (half a degree a press). Turning eases.
 5. The table rests on a triangle of feet that leaves every foot on or above the
    floor and holds the centre. A foot stands on the floor, or on whatever lying on
    the floor it came down onto -- a marble under a foot holds the table up, and is
    not crushed out from under it. With two such triangles it rocks between them as a
    leaned-on table does: smoothly, lingering a little at each rest. The page's
    clock runs at five sixths of real time, so everything happens as it would, a
    little slower: about 2.3 s there and back, a fall from the table about 0.28 s.
    With reduced motion it rests on the first. No foot ever goes below the floor.
    Motion, the turn's easing with it, is worked in fixed steps of 1/240 s of the
    page's clock, so it is the same at any frame rate; when nothing moves, the
    page draws nothing until something does.
 6. From above, the top is shaded by its tilt: a light wash on the high side,
    darker and more opaque toward the low side, deeper with steeper tilt, never
    opaque. The table is rigid, its top 0.67 above its feet (legs 0.6, the top 0.07
    thick), so tipped it leans: from above, the top sits off its feet downhill by
    its height times its tilt, where it is, and a side shows only where the tilt
    turns it up into view, a step deeper. The legs show through the top: round,
    0.085 across, each from its foot up to the top, leaning as the table leans.
    Under each foot lies a soft shadow on the floor, darkest where it stands,
    fading and widening as it lifts.
 7. Two cameras, a and b, each a short line and a letter on the plan, on the side
    it looks from; they turn with the table. Their elevations show the floor's
    section at every foot's depth, back to front, each foot on its own ground with
    its shadow as the plan has it, seen nearly edge-on (a soft darkening of the
    ground just under it); the legs round, perpendicular to the top, leaning with
    its tilt, a lifted foot's round end showing when the table tips away; the top
    as an opaque slab showing its
    top or its underside, whichever faces the camera, shaded along its tilt by the
    plan's own ramp, the sides and underside a little deeper. Grounds, legs,
    marbles, eggs and pools are drawn back to front, and each is hidden by exactly
    the part of the slab nearer the camera than it, so nothing appears or vanishes
    except by moving. The floor's relief is drawn at twice its size, and the table
    is one rigid piece in that drawing. A body keeps its true size and shape: it
    is drawn from the point it rests on, by the floor's map or the table's (between
    them in the air, as it is between them), turned by the difference between the
    drawn slope there and the true one, and each of its points raised with depth as
    the drawn surface under it rises toward the camera, so it sits on the drawn
    surface as it sits on the true one (a plate on a tipped top, on its face, not
    into it). Each foot has the floor's section at its depth, the whole width of
    the view; so does each body off the table, at its own depth (what lies within 4
    mm of one depth sharing one), and the white on the floor at its own, each hidden
    by whatever is nearer, so nothing stands on air and the ground moves with what
    moves on it, unbroken. A body's section comes and goes over 0.4 s, as the body
    comes onto the floor and as it goes down the hole or onto a plate; the white's
    takes over its egg's; and a body leaving the world at the plan's edge fades away
    with its section over its last 5 cm. The hole is shown as if seen through the
    ground, as the legs are through the top in the plan: its cut through its middle,
    straight down to its bottom, is drawn first, in its colour (item 19), and no
    section of the ground is drawn over it; what stands above the floor, a leg or a
    body, is drawn over it whatever its depth, and what is down it, in it -- so
    nothing comes and goes there as the table turns, nor seems to go down it that
    has not. Each ground is in the plan's three washes, banded at the same heights,
    so a colour is a height in every view, as the top's shade is.
 8. A screen reader hears "It is still.", "It rocks.", "One foot is in the air."
    or "N feet are in the air.", then, for a moment after one, "Somebody
    sneezed.", then what is on the table ("2 marbles, 1 meatball and 1 plate of
    spaghetti are on the table."), how many roll on the floor, rest in a hollow,
    went down the hole, rolled away, rolled out the door (meatballs), broke, and
    are cracked.
 9. The label's second line says what is here: "a table, a floor", "a hole" once
    it has opened, then whatever else is ("7 marbles and an egg", "a plate of spaghetti", "a broken
    egg"), as it comes and goes. Once anything has gone down the hole or a meatball
    out the door, a line under "Turn it." counts it ("3 in the hole. 1 meatball out
    the door."); the counts are kept for the visit, through another floor. The
    label gives the page's own size, read live, and one line: "Turn it." The
    title (the tab, and any preview of a link) is "It's not your table that's
    wobbly." The console gives the Greek
    word for table, and what it means.
10. Colours come from the house tokens; a missing token falls back to ink or
    paper. Things with colours of their own keep their daylight colours in either
    theme, as the house defines them by day: white things (the white of an egg, the
    china of a plate, cheese) the light paper and rule; food (the yolk, the pasta and
    its strands, the sauce, the meatball) its glazes and ink. Dark mode follows the system until the theme button ("dark" or
    "light", the last word in the row of buttons) turns it for the visit; nothing
    is kept. A change of theme fades over half a second along the house's curve,
    the drawing with the page. Resizing redraws.
11. Marbles, at true size: glass spheres 16 mm across, 5.4 g (a reach is taken
    as 40 cm). Clicking the plan lays one just there, on the top or the floor;
    "drop a marble" lays one somewhere on the top. Each lays one more, at rest,
    just touching what it is laid on: over a plate, on its spaghetti or its china,
    come down onto from above to where it first touches; where something else
    already lies, at the nearest clear place beside it (looking out in rings 1.6 mm
    apart, a plate's 8 mm apart, so that it may look across the top), never under a
    plate's rim, and on the floor clear of the legs, the top and the hole (what goes
    down it must roll there); where there is no clear place, nothing is laid. One
    time in thirty it is an egg (item 16);
    one time in sixty, a plate of spaghetti (item 17).
12. One world moves them all: rigid bodies in their true shapes under gravity,
    and, wherever two things touch (two bodies, a body and the floor, the top or
    a leg), a push that keeps them from passing into each other and gives back a
    part of the speed they met at, the lesser of the two sides' (glass 0.9, wood
    0.6, china 0.5, egg 0.3, meatball 0.1, spaghetti 0.05; slower than 8 cm/s,
    nothing); Coulomb friction, the geometric mean of the two sides' (glass,
    wood and egg 0.4, china 0.35, meatball and spaghetti 0.6); rolling
    resistance, the larger of the two sides' levers (a marble's or an egg's 0.056
    mm, so a marble on the top's hard wood holds still on a slope gentler than
    about 0.4 degrees; the floor's 0.12 mm, for it gives a little under what rolls
    on it -- an estimate, between what a hard wood floor and a carpet measure -- so
    on it about 0.9 degrees; a meatball's 1.5 mm, 3 in 100); and the twist
    friction of the contact's patch. Contacts are found
    between the true shapes; the solver's rounds go on until they agree, at most
    ten, and forty where a plate presses on lighter things. Moving air drags
    on each body by its drag coefficient and frontal area. A marble rolls down a slope at five-sevenths of
    gravity times it; on a turning table it rolls, gaining two-sevenths of what
    the top beneath it gains, and does not simply ride; past an edge it rolls over
    it and falls, bounces, rolls on until it rests in a hollow or leaves the map.
    The table is driven, by hand and by the lean, and far heavier than what is on
    it. The world moves in the page clock's steps, each cut into as many as 24 for
    anything fast, enough that nothing moves more than 0.8 mm in one against
    whatever it might meet (up to 4.6 m/s): a marble riding a turning top moves
    hardly at all against it; bodies that cannot touch within a step are stepped
    apart. A body still for half a second, on
    nothing moving, rests until something moving touches it, and is woken before
    that step begins, so it takes the whole step, its weight and what it stands on
    with it; something still that touches it leaves it at rest, and it holds as the
    floor does. A marble or an egg is one
    glaze on the table and another once it has left it, in every view: Jun blue
    and crimson at first (item 15).
13. Each elevation is a camera with a frame: nothing in it is drawn outside its
    panel, and a body out of shot is simply not in that view.
14. The favicon is the table from above, in amber, lighter on dark tab bars.
15. A dot after the theme button, last in the row, turns the glazes. The floor, the
    table and the marbles each take one of the three families, and a press moves
    each to the next: celadon floor, amber table, Jun marbles (blue on the table,
    crimson off it); then a Jun floor, a Ru table, tenmoku marbles (rust and
    gold); then a tenmoku floor, a purple-splash table, celadon marbles (Longquan
    and qingbai); and round again. Eggs wear the marbles' glazes, painted in the
    wash of that family's middle glaze. The dot wears the middle glaze of the
    floor to come; a screen reader hears that family's name. The drawing fades
    from one to the next as it does for the theme; nothing is kept; without
    script there is no dot.
16. Eggs, at true size: a hen's egg, Hugelschaffer's curve turned about its axis,
    57 mm long and 44 across, widest 3 mm toward the blunt end; a shell 0.35 mm
    thick round its white and yolk, 62.9 g in all. Each is laid on its side, turned
    anyhow, painted with two bands and a zigzag between them. Some eggs are boiled
    and some are not, and how the universe lays them so remains a mystery; the
    page does not say which. A boiled egg is one rigid body. A raw egg's shell
    carries its liquid round its axis only by viscosity (a raw egg stopped and let
    go turns again, for about a second), and across its axis partly by its walls,
    as a spheroid of its proportions does, the rest by viscosity again. An egg
    rolls in arcs, round the apex of the cone that touches it where it lies. Met
    as hard as a fall of 1 cm onto a hard floor, it cracks where it was met; a raw
    one met as hard as a fall of 20 cm breaks open. On the floor its white is a
    liquid: a thin film flowing over the floor's relief as thin films do, a
    Bingham fluid that is still until the stress at the floor passes its yield
    (0.5 Pa; whole white measures below 0.8) and then flows with a plastic
    viscosity of 0.1 Pa s (an estimate: egg white thins as it is sheared, and a
    creeping film shears slowly); 33 cubic centimetres, its depth kept on cells 8
    mm a side. It starts as a splash would leave it, 10 cm across (the splash is
    not followed), runs downhill and into hollows, and stops where it has thinned
    to its yield: on a level floor 17 cm across and 3 mm deep, as a static
    Bingham puddle is. The yolk and the bits of shell ride its surface. On
    another floor it flows again, on that one. Nothing rolling through it feels
    it, and it flows under the legs as if they were not there. On the top, where
    no egg breaks but by something a hand could not do, the white is drawn,
    spreading over half a second.
17. On top of spaghetti, all covered with cheese: a dinner plate, china, 26 cm
    across, its well 16 cm, its rim rising to 2.4 cm; on it a heap of spaghetti in
    sauce, 4 cm high, slumping into the plate as a heap does, its strands, sauce
    and cheese drawn on it; plate and heap 973 g, one rigid body, standing on the
    rim of its foot, riding the table by friction and sliding if the table is
    turned hard. The top and the floor meet it, whichever way up it is, at 48
    points round its foot and 48 round its lip (lying flat, every third of them
    carries it) and at its heap's crown and twelve points round that, the top at
    each point's nearest on it -- face, side or edge, and from inside, the shortest
    way out -- and, near its edge, at their nearest; so a plate set past the edge
    tips over it and falls, and may land upside down, and none goes into the top
    by half a millimetre. Seen from beneath, it is its china alone; from the side,
    its heap shows beyond its near rim, its own way up. A sphere meets it by its true section, so
    a marble can tuck under the rim's outer centimetre; an egg meets its top from
    above and its outline from beside. On the heap a meatball, 4 cm across, 35 g,
    set just so: where the plate's lean leaves it 99 in 100 of the way to
    rolling. In two and a half to five seconds somebody sneezes: from uphill of
    it, 30 cm off and 30 cm up, a round turbulent jet at 4.5 m/s from a mouth 2 cm
    across (as measured by schlieren, 2013), keeping that speed for six widths,
    then falling as one over the distance, a gust of 0.35 s where its front
    arrives. It pushes the meatball with about a six-hundredth of its weight --
    enough at its edge, and nothing a marble or an egg on the table would notice
    -- and the meatball rolls down the heap, over the rim, off the table, and
    rolls on as the floor lets it; out of the map, it rolled out the door. On a
    table that rocks, it may go before anyone sneezes. Only the meatball rolls:
    the pasta, the sauce and the cheese stay put, as at that gust they would.
18. "from a leg", before the theme button, flips the plan's square into the table's frame
    ("from above" flips it back): the eye rides the leg farthest from both cameras'
    marks, on its inner side half way up, looking at the table's middle and a little
    down, and turns and rocks with the table, so the table holds still and the world
    moves round it. In true perspective it sees the same things: the underside of the
    top, shaded along its tilt as every view shades the top; the floor in the plan's
    washes, meeting along the heights where they change as in the plan, and its
    contours, drawn finer where it is near, so no edge of it is coarse; the other
    legs, round, with their shadows; and everything below the top or beyond its
    edge -- what lies on the top is hidden by it. The room goes on past the plan's
    square, and far things fade into the paper, from 80 cm off to 1.6 m, so its
    edge is never seen; a body nearing the plan's edge, where it leaves the world,
    fades away over its last 5 cm. The hole is as the plan has it, and what
    is down it is seen through it. Far things are drawn before near ones, so a hill
    hides what is behind it, and each body after the floor it stands on. Dragging
    sideways turns the table; a click lays a marble where the eye's ray meets the floor,
    or, meeting the underside, on the top above that point. Without script there is no
    button.
19. A hole in the floor, 5 cm across -- room for a meatball, or an egg on its side
    -- and 7 cm deep, so even an egg on its end goes all the way down; its bottom
    level, its wall straight down from the floor's edge round it. It opens once there
    are three marbles in the room, and each new floor waits for its own: somewhere
    on a slope of at least 3 in 100, so never in a hollow, beyond where the legs can
    stand, inside the plan, and clear of whatever lies there; it comes into its
    colour from the floor's own over a second. It is a hole and not a
    mark: the floor is not there, and its edge is met where it is nearest. A marble
    whose touch crosses onto it is drawn in by the edge and drops (one rolling at
    0.24 m/s, 22.5 mm off its middle, does); one whose touch stays on the floor goes
    by; rolling straight at it, one at 0.8 m/s drops in and one at 1.2 m/s hops it.
    The wall and the bottom hold what is down it, and it can fill. A plate rests
    across it on its foot. White flowing to its edge pours in and is gone. It is the
    glaze of the floor's third where it is, deeper than the floor round it: by day
    that glaze's base; by night its wash two thirds of the way to the paper. In the
    plan it is so, with what is in it seen from above; in the elevations as item 7
    has it; from a leg, item 18. Whatever has gone all the way below the floor
    there is counted, once (item 9).
